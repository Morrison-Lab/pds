1 The Bernoulli distribution

ExmExample 1 (A coin flip)

Example 1 (A coin flip) The Bernoulli distribution describes a binary outcome. The indicator of heads on one flip of a fair coin is \(\operatorname{Ber}(1/2)\). By the expectation of the Bernoulli distribution, \(X \sim \operatorname{Ber}(\pi)\) has mean \(\pi\), and by the variance of a Bernoulli random variable, it has variance \(\pi(1 - \pi)\).

2 The binomial distribution

DefDefinition 1 (Binomial distribution)

Definition 1 (Binomial distribution) A discrete random variable \(X\) has the binomial distribution with number of trials \(n \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\) and event probability \(\pi\in [0, 1]\), written \(X \sim {\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(n, \pi\right)\mathclose{}}\) [1], if:

\[ \mathbb{P}(X = x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\binom{n}{x} \pi^x (1 - \pi)^{n - x}, \quad x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, \dots, n\right\}\mathclose{}} \tag{1}\]

where \(\binom{n}{x} = \frac{n!}{x!\,(n - x)!}\) is the number of ways to choose \(x\) of the \(n\) trials.

RemarkRemark

Remark. Casella and Berger [2, Sec. 3.2] defines the binomial distribution the same way. With \(n = 1\), Equation 1 is the Bernoulli PMF: \({\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(1, \pi\right)\mathclose{}}\) and \(\operatorname{Ber}(\pi)\) are the same distribution.

ExmExample 2 (Two heads in three flips)

Example 2 (Two heads in three flips) Let \(X \sim {\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(3, 1/2\right)\mathclose{}}\) be the number of heads in three flips of a fair coin. By Equation 1:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X = 2) &= \binom{3}{2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^2 \mathopen{}\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^{3 - 2} && \text{(binomial PMF with } n = 3,\ \pi= 1/2,\ x = 2 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{3!}{2!\,1!} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^2 \mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^1 && \text{(definition of } \tbinom{3}{2} \text{; } 1 - 1/2 = 1/2 \text{)} \\ &= 3 \cdot\frac{1}{4} \cdot\frac{1}{2} && \text{(evaluate the factorials and powers)} \\ &= \frac{3}{8} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

The three outcomes with two heads are \(HHT\), \(HTH\), and \(THH\), each with probability \(1/8\).

ExmExample 3 (Draws without replacement are not binomial)

Example 3 (Draws without replacement are not binomial) An urn holds two red balls and two blue balls. Draw two balls without replacement, and let \(X\) be the number of red balls drawn. Each draw is red with probability \(1/2\), but \(X \not\sim {\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(2, 1/2\right)\mathclose{}}\), because the draws are not independent:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X = 2) &= \mathbb{P}(\text{first red}) \cdot\mathbb{P}(\text{second red} \mid \text{first red}) && \text{(multiplication rule)} \\ &= \frac{2}{4} \cdot\frac{1}{3} && \text{(one red ball is left among three)} \\ &= \frac{1}{6} && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

whereas, by Equation 1, a \({\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(2, 1/2\right)\mathclose{}}\) random variable has

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X = 2) &= \binom{2}{2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^2 \mathopen{}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^0 && \text{(binomial PMF with } n = 2,\ \pi= 1/2,\ x = 2 \text{)} \\ &= \frac{1}{4} && \text{(evaluate)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 1 (A sum of IID Bernoulli variables is binomial)

Theorem 1 (A sum of IID Bernoulli variables is binomial) If \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are IID \(\operatorname{Ber}(\pi)\) random variables, then their sum \(S = \sum_{i=1}^nX_i\) has the \({\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(n, \pi\right)\mathclose{}}\) distribution.

ProofProof

Proof. Each \(X_i\) is \(0\) or \(1\), so \(S\) takes values in \({\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, \dots, n\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Let \(x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, \dots, n\right\}\mathclose{}}\) be a value of \(S\), and let \(A_x\) be the set of sequences \((s_1, \ldots, s_n) \in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1\right\}\mathclose{}}^n\) with exactly \(x\) ones. The event \({\mathopen{}\left\{S = x\right\}\mathclose{}}\) is the disjoint union of the events \({\mathopen{}\left\{X_1 = s_1, \ldots, X_n = s_n\right\}\mathclose{}}\) over \((s_1, \ldots, s_n) \in A_x\). For one such sequence:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X_1 = s_1, \ldots, X_n = s_n) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \mathbb{P}(X_i = s_i) && \text{(independence)} \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n \pi^{s_i} (1 - \pi)^{1 - s_i} && \text{(Bernoulli PMF)} \\ &= \pi^{\sum_{i=1}^ns_i} (1 - \pi)^{\sum_{i=1}^n(1 - s_i)} && \text{(collect exponents)} \\ &= \pi^x (1 - \pi)^{n - x} && \text{(} \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^ns_i = x \text{ for a sequence in } A_x \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Choosing which \(x\) of the \(n\) positions hold the ones determines the sequence, so \(A_x\) has \(\binom{n}{x}\) elements. Therefore:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(S = x) &= \sum_{(s_1, \ldots, s_n) \in A_x} \mathbb{P}(X_1 = s_1, \ldots, X_n = s_n) && \text{(additivity over disjoint events)} \\ &= \sum_{(s_1, \ldots, s_n) \in A_x} \pi^x (1 - \pi)^{n - x} && \text{(probability of one sequence)} \\ &= \binom{n}{x} \pi^x (1 - \pi)^{n - x} && \text{(} A_x \text{ has } \tbinom{n}{x} \text{ elements)} \end{aligned} \]

which is Equation 1.

ExmExample 4 (Counting treatment responders)

Example 4 (Counting treatment responders) Suppose five patients each respond to a treatment with probability \(0.2\), independently of one another, and let \(X_i = 1\) if patient \(i\) responds and \(X_i = 0\) otherwise. By Theorem 1, the number of responders \(S = \sum_{i=1}^5 X_i\) is \({\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(5, 0.2\right)\mathclose{}}\), so the probability that nobody responds is:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(S = 0) &= \binom{5}{0} (0.2)^0 (1 - 0.2)^{5 - 0} && \text{(binomial PMF with } n = 5,\ \pi= 0.2,\ x = 0 \text{)} \\ &= 1 \cdot 1 \cdot(0.8)^5 && \text{(} \tbinom{5}{0} = 1 \text{ and } (0.2)^0 = 1 \text{)} \\ &= 0.32768 && \text{(evaluate the power)} \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 2 (Mean and variance of the binomial distribution)

Theorem 2 (Mean and variance of the binomial distribution) If \(X \sim {\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(n, \pi\right)\mathclose{}}\), then:

  • \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}} = n\pi\)
  • \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X\right)\mathclose{}} = n\pi(1 - \pi)\)
ProofProof

Proof. The mean and variance of \(X\) depend only on its distribution, so by Theorem 1 we can compute them for \(S = \sum_{i=1}^nX_i\), where \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) are IID \(\operatorname{Ber}(\pi)\). For the mean, apply linearity of expectation \(n - 1\) times, then the Bernoulli mean:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[S\right]\mathclose{}} &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right]\mathclose{}} && \text{(definition of } S \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X_i\right]\mathclose{}} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\pi && \text{(Bernoulli mean)} \\ &= n\pi && \text{(sum of } n \text{ copies of } \pi\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

For the variance, apply the variance of a linear combination with \(a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 1\), then independent components give a diagonal variance matrix, then the Bernoulli variance:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\mathclose{}} &= \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n {\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(variance of a linear combination with } a_i = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n{\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(} {\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{}} = 0 \text{ for } i \neq j \text{, by independence)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X_i\right)\mathclose{}} && \text{(} {\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_i\right)\mathclose{}} = {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X_i\right)\mathclose{}} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\pi(1 - \pi) && \text{(Bernoulli variance)} \\ &= n\pi(1 - \pi) && \text{(sum of } n \text{ copies of } \pi(1 - \pi) \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 5 (Expected number of responders)

Example 5 (Expected number of responders) In Example 4, \(S \sim {\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\left(5, 0.2\right)\mathclose{}}\). By Theorem 2:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[S\right]\mathclose{}} &= 5 \cdot 0.2 && \text{(} n\pi\text{)} \\ &= 1 && \text{(multiply)} \\ {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S\right)\mathclose{}} &= 5 \cdot 0.2 \cdot(1 - 0.2) && \text{(} n\pi(1 - \pi) \text{)} \\ &= 0.8 && \text{(multiply)} \end{aligned} \]

On average, one of the five patients responds.

3 The Poisson distribution

DefDefinition 2 (Poisson distribution)

Definition 2 (Poisson distribution) A random variable \(Y\) has the Poisson distribution with mean parameter \(\mu> 0\), written \(Y \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\) [3], if:

\[\mathbb{P}(Y = y) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\mu^{y} e^{-\mu}}{y!}, \quad y \in \mathbb{N}= {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}} \tag{2}\]

LemTheorem 3 (CDF of Poisson distribution)

Theorem 3 (CDF of Poisson distribution) If \(Y \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\), then for every real number \(y\):

\[\mathbb{P}(Y \le y) = e^{-\mu} \sum_{j=0}^{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor y\right\rfloor\mathclose{}}\frac{\mu^j}{j!} \tag{3}\]

ProofProof

Proof. For \(y < 0\), both sides are \(0\) (the sum is empty). For any \(y \ge 0\), the event \(\{Y \le y\}\) is the disjoint union of events \(\{Y = j\}\) for all non-negative integers \(j \le \mathopen{}\left\lfloor y\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\). Applying the Poisson PMF (Equation 2) and factoring out the common term \(e^{-\mu}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Y \le y) &= \sum_{j=0}^{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor y\right\rfloor\mathclose{}} \mathbb{P}(Y = j) && (\text{disjoint union of events } Y = j) \\ &= \sum_{j=0}^{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor y\right\rfloor\mathclose{}} \frac{\mu^j e^{-\mu}}{j!} && (\text{definition of Poisson PMF}) \\ &= e^{-\mu} \sum_{j=0}^{\mathopen{}\left\lfloor y\right\rfloor\mathclose{}} \frac{\mu^j}{j!} && (\text{factoring out } e^{-\mu} \text{ constant wrt } j) \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 6 (Computing Poisson cumulative probabilities)

Example 6 (Computing Poisson cumulative probabilities) For a Poisson random variable \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu= 2)\), the probability of observing at most 2 events is computed as:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(X \le 2) &= e^{-2} \sum_{j=0}^{2} \frac{2^j}{j!} && (\text{apply CDF formula with } \mu= 2, y = 2) \\ &= e^{-2} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{2^0}{0!} + \frac{2^1}{1!} + \frac{2^2}{2!}\right)\mathclose{} && (\text{expand terms for } j = 0, 1, 2) \\ &= e^{-2} \mathopen{}\left(1 + 2 + 2\right)\mathclose{} && (\text{simplify factorials and powers}) \\ &= 5 e^{-2} \approx 0.677 && (\text{evaluate numerical value}) \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 4 (Mean and variance of the Poisson distribution)

Theorem 4 (Mean and variance of the Poisson distribution) If \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\), then:

  • \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \mu\)
  • \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \mu\)
ProofProof

Proof. Mean.

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[X] &= \sum_{x=0}^\infty x \cdot \mathbb{P}(X=x) && (\text{definition of expected value}) \\ &= 0 \cdot \mathbb{P}(X=0) + \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x \cdot \mathbb{P}(X=x) && (\text{separate } x=0 \text{ term}) \\ &= \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{x!} && (\text{substitute Poisson PMF}) \\ &= \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{x \cdot (x-1)!} && (\text{definition of factorial } x!) \\ &= \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{(x-1)!} && (\text{cancel factor of } x) \\ &= \mu\cdot \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu^{x-1} e^{-\mu}}{(x-1)!} && (\text{factor out one power of } \mu) \\ &= \mu\cdot \sum_{y=0}^\infty \frac{\mu^y e^{-\mu}}{y!} && (\text{change index variable } y \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}x-1) \\ &= \mu\cdot 1 && (\text{PMF sums to 1 over state space}) \\ &= \mu&& (\text{simplify}) \end{aligned} \]

Variance. The same steps, canceling two factors instead of one, give \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X(X-1)\right]\mathclose{}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X(X-1)\right]\mathclose{}} &= \sum_{x=2}^\infty x(x-1) \cdot \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{x!} && (\text{LOTUS; the } x = 0, 1 \text{ terms are } 0) \\ &= \sum_{x=2}^\infty \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu}}{(x-2)!} && (\text{cancel } x(x-1) \text{ against } x!) \\ &= \mu^2 \cdot \sum_{y=0}^\infty \frac{\mu^y e^{-\mu}}{y!} && (\text{factor out } \mu^2 \text{; } y \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}x - 2) \\ &= \mu^2 && (\text{PMF sums to 1}) \end{aligned} \]

Then, by the simplified expression for variance and linearity of expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X^2\right]\mathclose{}} - {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{2} && (\text{simplified expression for variance}) \\ &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X(X-1)\right]\mathclose{}} + {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}} - {\mathopen{}\left({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}}\right)\mathclose{}}^{2} && (X^2 = X(X-1) + X \text{; linearity}) \\ &= \mu^2 + \mu- \mu^2 && (\text{substitute}) \\ &= \mu&& (\text{simplify}) \end{aligned} \]

See also https://statproofbook.github.io/P/poiss-mean and https://statproofbook.github.io/P/poiss-var.

LemTheorem 5 (Ratio of consecutive Poisson probabilities, and the mode)

Theorem 5 (Ratio of consecutive Poisson probabilities, and the mode) If \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\), then:

  • \(\mathbb{P}(X=x) = \frac{\mu}{x} \mathbb{P}(X = x-1)\) for \(x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\)
  • For \(x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\) with \(x < \mu\), \(\mathbb{P}(X=x) > \mathbb{P}(X = x-1)\)
  • For \(x = \mu\) (possible only when \(\mu\) is an integer), \(\mathbb{P}(X=x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x-1)\)
  • For \(x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\) with \(x > \mu\), \(\mathbb{P}(X=x) < \mathbb{P}(X = x-1)\)
  • If \(\mu\) is not an integer, \(\arg \max_{x} \mathbb{P}(X=x) = \mathopen{}\left\lfloor\mu\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\); if \(\mu\) is an integer, the maximum is attained at both \(x = \mu- 1\) and \(x = \mu\)
ProofProof

Proof. Ratio of consecutive probabilities. For \(x \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\mathbb{P}(X = x)}{\mathbb{P}(X = x - 1)} &= \frac{\mu^x e^{-\mu} / x!}{\mu^{x-1} e^{-\mu} / (x-1)!} && (\text{substitute Poisson PMF}) \\ &= \frac{\mu}{x} && (\text{cancel } \mu^{x-1} e^{-\mu} \text{ and } (x-1)!) \end{aligned} \]

This ratio is greater than, equal to, or less than 1 as \(x\) is less than, equal to, or greater than \(\mu\), which gives the three comparisons. So \(\mathbb{P}(X = x)\) increases while \(x < \mu\) and decreases once \(x > \mu\). If \(\mu\) is not an integer, the last increase is at \(x = \mathopen{}\left\lfloor\mu\right\rfloor\mathclose{}\), which is therefore the unique mode. If \(\mu\) is an integer, \(\mathbb{P}(X = \mu) = \mathbb{P}(X = \mu- 1)\), and both are modes.

ExmExample 7 (Mode of a Poisson distribution)

Example 7 (Mode of a Poisson distribution) For \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(2.5)\), \(\mathbb{P}(X = 2) = \frac{2.5}{2}\mathbb{P}(X = 1) > \mathbb{P}(X = 1)\) and \(\mathbb{P}(X = 3) = \frac{2.5}{3}\mathbb{P}(X = 2) < \mathbb{P}(X = 2)\), so the mode is \(\mathopen{}\left\lfloor 2.5\right\rfloor\mathclose{} = 2\). For \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(2)\), \(\mathbb{P}(X = 2) = \frac{2}{2}\mathbb{P}(X = 1)\), so \(1\) and \(2\) are both modes, each with probability \(2 e^{-2} \approx 0.271\).

DefDefinition 3 (Exposure magnitude)

Definition 3 (Exposure magnitude) For many count outcomes, there is some sense of an exposure magnitude, such as population size or duration of observation, which multiplicatively rescales the expected (mean) count.

Solution.

RemarkRemark

Remark. Exposure units are similar to the number of trials in a binomial distribution, but in non-binomial count outcomes, there can be more than one event per unit of exposure.

We can use \(t\) to represent continuous-valued exposures/observation durations, and \(n\) to represent discrete-valued exposures.

DefDefinition 4 (Event rate)

Definition 4 (Event rate) For a count \(Y\) observed over a fixed, known exposure magnitude \(t > 0\), the event rate, denoted \({\lambda}\), is the mean of \(Y\) divided by the exposure magnitude:

\[{\lambda}\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}}}{t} \tag{4}\]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The event rate is a transformation of the mean: it removes the exposure magnitude from the mean, so counts observed over different exposures can be compared on one scale. Regression models for counts use the same decomposition, with the rate depending on covariates and the exposure magnitude known (see rme’s count-regression chapter).

LemTheorem 6 (Transformation function from event rate to mean)

Theorem 6 (Transformation function from event rate to mean) If a count \(Y\) is observed over exposure magnitude \(t > 0\) with event rate \({\lambda}\), then its mean \(\mu\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}}\) is:

\[\mu= {\lambda}\cdot t \tag{5}\]

ProofProof

Proof. By Definition 4:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\lambda}&\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}}}{t} && (\text{definition of event rate}) \\ {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}} &= {\lambda}\cdot t && (\text{multiply both sides by } t > 0) \\ \mu&= {\lambda}\cdot t && (\mu\stackrel{\text{def}}{=}{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}}) \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 8 (Calculating expected counts from event rates)

Example 8 (Calculating expected counts from event rates) Suppose a city records a disease event rate of \({\lambda}= 0.05\) cases per person-year. For a subpopulation with an exposure magnitude of \(t = 100\) person-years, the expected count of cases is, by Theorem 6:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu&= {\lambda}\cdot t && (\text{transformation from event rate to mean}) \\ &= 0.05 \times 100 && (\text{substitute } {\lambda}= 0.05 \text{ and } t = 100) \\ &= 5 \text{ cases} && (\text{evaluate expected count}) \end{aligned} \]

LemTheorem 7 (No exposure means no expected events)

Theorem 7 (No exposure means no expected events) For each exposure magnitude \(t \ge 0\), let \(Y_t\) be the count observed over exposure \(t\). If the mean count is proportional to the exposure magnitude, \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_t\right]\mathclose{}} = {\lambda}\cdot t\) for all \(t \ge 0\) with one finite rate \({\lambda}\), then there are no expected events without exposure:

\[{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_0\right]\mathclose{}} = 0\]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_0\right]\mathclose{}} &= {\lambda}\cdot 0 && (\text{evaluate } {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_t\right]\mathclose{}} = {\lambda}t \text{ at } t = 0) \\ &= 0 && (\text{multiplication by zero; } {\lambda}\text{ is finite}) \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The hypothesis carries the content here. Definition 4 alone cannot give \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_0\right]\mathclose{}} = 0\), since \({\lambda}= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}}/t\) is undefined at \(t = 0\); the result holds for a model that assumes one rate \({\lambda}\) shared across exposure magnitudes, including \(t = 0\).

ExmExample 9 (Zero exposure time)

Example 9 (Zero exposure time) If a subject is observed for \(t = 0\) person-years, no follow-up time has elapsed, so under a constant-rate model the expected number of incident events is \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_0\right]\mathclose{}} = 0\).

Important

A mean proportional to exposure, \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_t\right]\mathclose{}} = {\lambda}t\), has no term that stays nonzero at \(t = 0\): a model of this form says that with no exposure, no events are expected. A mean with an added constant, such as \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y_t\right]\mathclose{}} = c + {\lambda}t\) with \(c > 0\), would expect \(c\) events even with no exposure. Regression models for counts keep the proportional form when they add covariates (see rme’s count-regression chapter).

LemTheorem 8 (Exposure is additive on the log scale)

Theorem 8 (Exposure is additive on the log scale) If \(\mu= {\lambda}\cdot t\) with \({\lambda}> 0\) and \(t > 0\), then:

\[\log{\mu} = \log{{\lambda}} + \log{t}\]

ProofProof

Proof. \[ \begin{aligned} \log{\mu} &= \log({\lambda}\cdot t) && (\text{substitute } \mu= {\lambda}\cdot t) \\ &= \log{{\lambda}} + \log{t} && (\text{logarithm product rule}) \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 10 (Log-linear representation of expected counts)

Example 10 (Log-linear representation of expected counts) If a clinic sees an event rate of \({\lambda}= 0.02\) events/day and \(t = 30\) days of observation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \log{\mu} &= \log(0.02) + \log(30) && (\text{apply log-scale formula}) \\ &= -3.912 + 3.401 && (\text{evaluate natural logarithms}) \\ &= -0.511 && (\text{sum terms}) \end{aligned} \]

Exponentiating yields \(\mu= {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-0.511\right\}\mathclose{}} \approx 0.60\) expected events.

DefDefinition 5 (Offset)

Definition 5 (Offset) In a model whose linear component contains a term with no unknown coefficient, that term is called an offset: it shifts the linear component by a known amount. In a count model with exposure magnitude \(t\), the offset is the known term \(\log{t}\) in the log-scale decomposition of the mean (8),

\[\log{\mu} = \log{{\lambda}} + \log{t}.\]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The offset needs no covariates: with a single exposure \(t\) and an unknown rate \({\lambda}\), \(\log{t}\) is already an offset. Regression models for counts keep the same term and add covariate terms beside it (see rme’s count-regression chapter).

ExmExample 11 (The offset for a clinic’s event count)

Example 11 (The offset for a clinic’s event count) In Example 10, the clinic was observed for \(t = 30\) days, so the offset is \(\log{30} \approx 3.401\). Only \(\log{{\lambda}}\) is unknown before the data are seen; the offset is fixed by the length of observation. A clinic observed for \(t = 60\) days has offset \(\log{60} \approx 4.094\), which raises \(\log{\mu}\) by \(\log{2} \approx 0.693\) at the same event rate.

LemTheorem 9 (Sum of independent Poisson random variables)

Theorem 9 (Sum of independent Poisson random variables) If \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent Poisson random variables with means \(\mu_X\) and \(\mu_Y\), their sum, \(Z=X+Y\), is also a Poisson random variable, with mean \(\mu_Z = \mu_X + \mu_Y\).

ProofProof

Proof. The event \(\{Z = z\}\) is the disjoint union of the events \(\{X = k, Y = z - k\}\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, z\) (since \(X\) and \(Y\) are non-negative integers), so:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Z = z) &= \sum_{k=0}^z \mathbb{P}(X = k,\, Y = z - k) && (\text{countable additivity}) \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^z \mathbb{P}(X = k) \mathbb{P}(Y = z - k) && (\text{independence}) \\ &= \sum_{k=0}^z \frac{\mu_X^k e^{-\mu_X}}{k!} \frac{\mu_Y^{z-k} e^{-\mu_Y}}{(z-k)!} && (\text{substitute Poisson PMFs}) \\ &= e^{-(\mu_X + \mu_Y)} \sum_{k=0}^z \frac{\mu_X^k \mu_Y^{z-k}}{k!(z-k)!} && (\text{factor out } e^{-\mu_X} e^{-\mu_Y}) \\ &= \frac{e^{-(\mu_X + \mu_Y)}}{z!} \sum_{k=0}^z \frac{z!}{k!(z-k)!} \mu_X^k \mu_Y^{z-k} && (\text{multiply and divide by } z!) \\ &= \frac{e^{-(\mu_X + \mu_Y)}}{z!} (\mu_X + \mu_Y)^z && (\text{binomial theorem}) \end{aligned} \]

This probability expression matches the PMF of a \(\operatorname{Pois}(\mu_X + \mu_Y)\) random variable (see also https://web.stanford.edu/class/archive/cs/cs109/cs109.1206/lectureNotes/LN12_independent_rvs.pdf, Example 3).

ExmExample 12 (Aggregating independent region counts)

Example 12 (Aggregating independent region counts) Suppose Region A records \(X \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu_X = 12)\) cases and Region B records \(Y \sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu_Y = 18)\) cases independently. By Theorem 9, the combined total count \(Z = X + Y\) follows a Poisson distribution:

\[ \begin{aligned} Z &\sim \operatorname{Pois}(\mu_X + \mu_Y) && (\text{sum of independent Poissons}) \\ &= \operatorname{Pois}(12 + 18) && (\text{substitute region means}) \\ &= \operatorname{Pois}(30) && (\text{evaluate sum}) \end{aligned} \]

4 The Negative-Binomial distribution

DefDefinition 6 (Negative binomial distribution)

Definition 6 (Negative binomial distribution) A random variable \(Y\) has the negative binomial distribution with mean \(\mu> 0\) and overdispersion parameter \(\rho> 0\), written \(Y \sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, \rho)\) [4], if, for \(y \in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(Y=y) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\mu^y}{y!} \cdot \frac{\Gamma(\rho+ y)}{\Gamma(\rho) \cdot (\rho+ \mu)^y} \cdot \mathopen{}\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{-\rho} \]

where \(\Gamma\) is the gamma function, which satisfies \(\Gamma(x) = (x-1)!\) for positive integers \(x\).

LemTheorem 10 (The negative binomial converges to the Poisson)

Theorem 10 (The negative binomial converges to the Poisson) Let the population mean be \(\mu> 0\), and let \(Y_\rho\sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, \rho)\). Then for each \(y \in {\mathopen{}\left\{0, 1, 2, \dots\right\}\mathclose{}}\), as \(\rho\rightarrow \infty\), the negative binomial PMF converges to the Poisson PMF (Equation 2):

\[\lim_{\rho\rightarrow \infty} \mathbb{P}(Y_\rho= y) = \frac{\mu^{y} e^{-\mu}}{y!}\]

ProofProof

Proof. Choose a value \(y\) of \(Y_\rho\). In Definition 6, the first factor \(\mu^y / y!\) does not depend on \(\rho\). For the second factor, \(\Gamma(\rho+ y) = \Gamma(\rho) \prod_{k=0}^{y-1} (\rho+ k)\), by applying \(\Gamma(x + 1) = x\,\Gamma(x)\) \(y\) times (for \(y = 0\), the product is empty and equals 1), so:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\Gamma(\rho+ y)}{\Gamma(\rho) \cdot (\rho+ \mu)^y} &= \frac{\Gamma(\rho) \prod_{k=0}^{y-1} (\rho+ k)}{\Gamma(\rho) \cdot (\rho+ \mu)^y} && (\Gamma(x + 1) = x\,\Gamma(x) \text{, applied } y \text{ times}) \\ &= \prod_{k=0}^{y-1} \frac{\rho+ k}{\rho+ \mu} && (\text{cancel } \Gamma(\rho) \text{; one factor of } \rho+ \mu\text{ per } k) \\ &= \prod_{k=0}^{y-1} \frac{1 + k/\rho}{1 + \mu/\rho} && (\text{divide each numerator and denominator by } \rho) \\ &\rightarrow \prod_{k=0}^{y-1} \frac{1 + 0}{1 + 0} && (k/\rho\rightarrow 0 \text{ and } \mu/\rho\rightarrow 0 \text{; finitely many factors}) \\ &= 1 && (\text{simplify}) \end{aligned} \]

For the third factor, write \(x \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\mu/\rho\), so \(x \rightarrow 0\) as \(\rho\rightarrow \infty\), and \(\rho= \mu/x\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \log\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left(1 + \frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{-\rho}\right)\mathclose{} &= -\rho\log\mathopen{}\left(1 + \frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)\mathclose{} && (\text{log of a power}) \\ &= -\mu\cdot \frac{\log(1 + x)}{x} && (\text{substitute } \rho= \mu/x) \\ &\rightarrow -\mu\cdot 1 && (\textstyle\lim_{x \rightarrow 0} \log(1 + x)/x = 1 \text{, the derivative of } \log(1 + x) \text{ at } x = 0) \\ &= -\mu && (\text{simplify}) \end{aligned} \]

so, since the exponential function is continuous, \(\mathopen{}\left(1 + \mu/\rho\right)\mathclose{}^{-\rho} \rightarrow {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\mu\right\}\mathclose{}}\). Each of the three factors has a limit, so their product does too:

\[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{\rho\rightarrow \infty} \mathbb{P}(Y_\rho= y) &= \frac{\mu^y}{y!} \cdot 1 \cdot {\operatorname{exp}\mathopen{}\left\{-\mu\right\}\mathclose{}} && (\text{limit of a product of convergent factors}) \\ &= \frac{\mu^{y} e^{-\mu}}{y!} && (\text{rearrange}) \end{aligned} \]

which is the \(\operatorname{Pois}(\mu)\) PMF.

LemTheorem 11 (Mean and variance of the negative binomial distribution)

Theorem 11 (Mean and variance of the negative binomial distribution) If \(Y \sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, \rho)\), then:

  • \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mu\)
  • \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} = \mu+ \frac{\mu^2}{\rho} > \mu\)
ProofProof

Proof. The negative binomial distribution is a gamma mixture of Poisson distributions: if \(\Lambda\) has the gamma density \(g({\lambda}) = \frac{(\rho/\mu)^\rho}{\Gamma(\rho)} {\lambda}^{\rho- 1} e^{-\rho{\lambda}/\mu}\) for \({\lambda}> 0\), which has mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\mu^2/\rho\) [2], and \(Y \mid \Lambda= {\lambda}\sim \operatorname{Pois}({\lambda})\), then \(Y \sim \operatorname{NegBin}(\mu, \rho)\). To check this, integrate the joint density over \({\lambda}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(Y = y) &= \int_0^\infty \frac{{\lambda}^y e^{-{\lambda}}}{y!} \cdot\frac{(\rho/\mu)^\rho}{\Gamma(\rho)} {\lambda}^{\rho- 1} e^{-\rho{\lambda}/\mu}\,d{\lambda} && (\text{marginalize over } \Lambda) \\ &= \frac{(\rho/\mu)^\rho}{y!\,\Gamma(\rho)} \int_0^\infty {\lambda}^{y + \rho- 1} e^{-{\lambda}(1 + \rho/\mu)}\,d{\lambda} && (\text{collect powers of } {\lambda}\text{ and exponents}) \\ &= \frac{(\rho/\mu)^\rho}{y!\,\Gamma(\rho)} \cdot\frac{\Gamma(y + \rho)}{(1 + \rho/\mu)^{y + \rho}} && (\textstyle\int_0^\infty {\lambda}^{a-1} e^{-b{\lambda}}\,d{\lambda}= \Gamma(a)/b^a) \\ &= \frac{\Gamma(y + \rho)}{y!\,\Gamma(\rho)} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\rho}{\mu}\right)\mathclose{}^\rho\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+ \rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{y + \rho} && (1 + \rho/\mu= (\mu+ \rho)/\mu) \\ &= \frac{\Gamma(y + \rho)}{y!\,\Gamma(\rho)} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\rho}{\mu+ \rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{\rho} \mathopen{}\left(\frac{\mu}{\mu+ \rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{y} && (\text{combine the } \mu^\rho\text{ factors}) \\ &= \frac{\mu^y}{y!} \cdot\frac{\Gamma(\rho+ y)}{\Gamma(\rho)\,(\rho+ \mu)^y} \cdot\mathopen{}\left(1 + \frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)\mathclose{}^{-\rho} && (\text{rearrange into the form of the definition}) \end{aligned} \]

Then, by the law of iterated expectations and the law of total variance, using

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y \mid \Lambda\right]\mathclose{}} &= \operatorname{Var}(Y \mid \Lambda) \\ &= \Lambda \end{aligned} \]

(Theorem 4):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[Y] &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y \mid \Lambda\right]\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{}} && (\text{law of iterated expectations}) \\ &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[\Lambda\right]\mathclose{}} && ({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y \mid \Lambda\right]\mathclose{}} = \Lambda) \\ &= \mu && (\text{mean of the gamma distribution}) \\ {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[{\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y \mid \Lambda\right)\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{}} + {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y \mid \Lambda\right]\mathclose{}}\right)\mathclose{}} && (\text{law of total variance}) \\ &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[\Lambda\right]\mathclose{}} + {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\Lambda\right)\mathclose{}} && ({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y \mid \Lambda\right)\mathclose{}} = \Lambda\text{ and } {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y \mid \Lambda\right]\mathclose{}} = \Lambda) \\ &= \mu+ \frac{\mu^2}{\rho} && (\text{mean and variance of the gamma distribution}) \end{aligned} \]

and \(\mu^2/\rho> 0\) gives \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} > \mu\).

ExmExample 13 (Overdispersion relative to the Poisson)

Example 13 (Overdispersion relative to the Poisson) With \(\mu= 4\) and \(\rho= 2\),

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} &= 4 + 16/2 \\ &= 12, \end{aligned} \]

three times the variance of a \(\operatorname{Pois}(4)\) count with the same mean.

5 The Laplace distribution

DefDefinition 7 (Laplace distribution)

Definition 7 (Laplace distribution) A random variable \(Y\) has the Laplace distribution with location parameter \(\mu\in \mathbb{R}\) and scale parameter \(b > 0\), written \(Y \sim {\operatorname{Laplace}\mathopen{}\left(\mu, b\right)\mathclose{}}\) [5], if \(Y\) is continuous with density:

\[\operatorname{p}(Y=y) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{2b} \text{e}^{-\frac{\mathopen{}\left|y - \mu\right|\mathclose{}}{b}}, \quad y \in \mathbb{R} \tag{6}\]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The normal density has the squared distance \((y - \mu)^2\) in its exponent. The Laplace density has the absolute distance \(\mathopen{}\left|y - \mu\right|\mathclose{}\) instead. Both densities are symmetric about \(\mu\), but the Laplace density has a sharp peak at \(\mu\) and decays more slowly in the tails.

ExmExample 14 (Laplace density values)

Example 14 (Laplace density values) Let \(Y \sim {\operatorname{Laplace}\mathopen{}\left(3, 2\right)\mathclose{}}\), so \(\mu= 3\) and \(b = 2\) (Definition 7). At the location, \(\mathopen{}\left|y - \mu\right|\mathclose{} = 0\), so the density is \(\frac{1}{2b}\). At a point \(y = 5\), the distance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathopen{}\left|5 - 3\right|\mathclose{} &= 2 \\ &= b, \end{aligned} \]

so the density is \(\frac{1}{2b}\text{e}^{-1}\). Here are both values:

b <- 2

  at_mu_plus_2 = exp(-1) / (2 * b)
) |>
  round(3)
#>        at_mu at_mu_plus_2 
#>        0.250        0.092

The density at \(\mu+ b\) is the density at \(\mu\) times \(\text{e}^{-1}\), and the same holds at \(\mu- b\), because the density depends only on the distance from \(\mu\).

LemTheorem 12 (Mean and variance of the Laplace distribution)

Theorem 12 (Mean and variance of the Laplace distribution) If \(Y \sim {\operatorname{Laplace}\mathopen{}\left(\mu, b\right)\mathclose{}}\) (Definition 7), then

\[{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}} = \mu\quad \text{and} \quad {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} = 2b^2. \tag{7}\]

ProofProof

Proof. Write \(U = Y - \mu\). By Equation 6, \(U\) has density \(\frac{1}{2b}\text{e}^{-\mathopen{}\left|u\right|\mathclose{}/b}\), which is symmetric about \(0\).

For the mean, the integrand \(u \cdot \frac{1}{2b}\text{e}^{-\mathopen{}\left|u\right|\mathclose{}/b}\) is an odd function, so its integral over \(\mathbb{R}\) is \(0\). Therefore \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[U\right]\mathclose{}} = 0\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[Y\right]\mathclose{}} &= \mu+ {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[U\right]\mathclose{}} \\ &= \mu. \end{aligned} \]

For the variance,

\[ \begin{aligned} {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(Y\right)\mathclose{}} &= {\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(U\right)\mathclose{}} \\ &= {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[U^2\right]\mathclose{}} \end{aligned} \]

because \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[U\right]\mathclose{}} = 0\). The integrand \(u^2 \cdot \frac{1}{2b}\text{e}^{-\mathopen{}\left|u\right|\mathclose{}/b}\) is even, so

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[U^2\right]\mathclose{}} &= 2 \int_0^\infty u^2 \frac{1}{2b} \text{e}^{-u/b} \, du && \text{(even integrand)} \\ &= \frac{1}{b} \int_0^\infty u^2 \text{e}^{-u/b} \, du && \text{(multiplying the constants)} \\ &= \frac{1}{b} \mathopen{}\left(2b \int_0^\infty u \text{e}^{-u/b} \, du\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(integration by parts; the boundary terms are $0$)} \\ &= \frac{1}{b} \mathopen{}\left(2b \cdot b^2\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(integration by parts again; the boundary terms are $0$)} \\ &= 2b^2. && \text{(multiplying the constants)} \end{aligned} \]

ExmExample 15 (Laplace and normal tails with the same mean and variance)

6 Weibull distribution

DefDefinition 8 (Weibull distribution)

Definition 8 (Weibull distribution) A non-negative random variable \(T\) has the Weibull distribution with shape \(\alpha> 0\) and rate \({\lambda}> 0\) [6] if its survival function is:

\[\operatorname{S}(t) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}, \quad t \ge 0\]

LemTheorem 13 (Weibull density and hazard)

Theorem 13 (Weibull density and hazard) If \(T\) has the Weibull distribution with shape \(\alpha\) and rate \({\lambda}\), then for \(t > 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &= \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}\\ \operatorname{h}(t) &= \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1} \end{aligned} \]

ProofProof

Proof. The CDF of \(T\) is \(F(t) = 1 - \operatorname{S}(t)\) (survival function and CDF), which is \(0\) for \(t < 0\) and \(1 - \text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}\) for \(t \ge 0\). This \(F\) is continuous everywhere, with a continuous derivative everywhere except possibly \(t = 0\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} f &= F' \\ &= -\operatorname{S}' \end{aligned} \]

is a density of \(T\) (a piecewise-smooth CDF has its derivative as a density). This density is continuous at every \(t > 0\), so there the hazard is \(f(t)/\operatorname{S}(t)\) (hazard equals density over survival):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &= -\frac{d}{dt}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha} && (f = -\operatorname{S}') \\ &= \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha} && (\text{chain rule}) \\ \operatorname{h}(t) &= \frac{\alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}}{\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}} && (\text{hazard is density over survival}) \\ &= \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1} && (\text{cancel}) \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The hazard is written \(\operatorname{h}(t)\) here, rather than \({\lambda}(t)\), because the Weibull rate parameter is also called \({\lambda}\).

LemTheorem 14 (Weibull mean)

Theorem 14 (Weibull mean) If \(T\) has the Weibull distribution with shape \(\alpha\) and rate \({\lambda}\), then:

\[{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} = \Gamma(1+1/\alpha)\cdot {\lambda}^{-1/\alpha}\]

ProofProof

Proof. For the mean, use the survival-function formula for the mean and substitute \(u = {\lambda}t^\alpha\), so that \(t = (u/{\lambda})^{1/\alpha}\) and \(dt = \frac{1}{\alpha}{\lambda}^{-1/\alpha} u^{1/\alpha- 1}\,du\):

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} &= \int_0^\infty \text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha}\,dt && (\text{expectation via the survival function}) \\ &= \frac{1}{\alpha}{\lambda}^{-1/\alpha} \int_0^\infty u^{1/\alpha- 1} \text{e}^{-u}\,du && (\text{substitute } u = {\lambda}t^\alpha) \\ &= \frac{1}{\alpha}{\lambda}^{-1/\alpha}\,\Gamma(1/\alpha) && (\text{definition of the gamma function}) \\ &= \Gamma(1 + 1/\alpha)\,{\lambda}^{-1/\alpha} && (\Gamma(1 + a) = a\,\Gamma(a)) \end{aligned} \]

CorCorollary 1 (The Weibull with shape 1 is the exponential)

Corollary 1 (The Weibull with shape 1 is the exponential) If \(T\) has the Weibull distribution with shape \(\alpha= 1\) and rate \({\lambda}\), then \(T\) has the exponential distribution with rate \({\lambda}\).

ProofProof

Proof. By Theorem 13 with \(\alpha= 1\), for \(t > 0\):

\[ \begin{aligned} f(t) &= \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^\alpha} && (\text{Weibull density}) \\ &= 1 \cdot {\lambda}t^{0}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t^1} && (\text{substitute } \alpha= 1) \\ &= {\lambda}\text{e}^{-{\lambda}t} && (t^0 = 1 \text{ and } t^1 = t) \end{aligned} \]

and \(f(t) = 0\) for \(t < 0\), since \(T\) is non-negative. This density matches the exponential density at every \(t \ne 0\). Changing a density at the single point \(t = 0\) does not change its integral over any set, so \(T\) has the exponential distribution with rate \({\lambda}\).

CorCorollary 2 (The Weibull shape sets the direction of the hazard)

Corollary 2 (The Weibull shape sets the direction of the hazard) If \(T\) has the Weibull distribution with shape \(\alpha\) and rate \({\lambda}\), then on \(t > 0\) its hazard \(\operatorname{h}(t)\) is:

  • strictly increasing if \(\alpha> 1\)
  • constant if \(\alpha= 1\)
  • strictly decreasing if \(\alpha< 1\)
ProofProof

Proof. By Theorem 13, \(\operatorname{h}(t) = \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1}\) for \(t > 0\), so:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{h}(t) &= \frac{d}{dt} \alpha{\lambda}t^{\alpha-1} && (\text{Weibull hazard}) \\ &= \alpha(\alpha- 1){\lambda}t^{\alpha-2} && (\text{power rule}) \end{aligned} \]

For \(t > 0\), the factors \(\alpha\), \({\lambda}\), and \(t^{\alpha-2}\) are all positive, so \(\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{h}(t)\) has the sign of \(\alpha- 1\): positive for \(\alpha> 1\), zero for \(\alpha= 1\), and negative for \(\alpha< 1\). A function with a positive (negative) derivative on an interval is strictly increasing (decreasing) there, and one with a zero derivative is constant.

RemarkRemark

Remark. The exponential distribution’s hazard is constant, so the Weibull’s choice of an increasing, constant, or decreasing hazard (Corollary 2) provides more flexibility than the exponential.

ExmExample 16 (Exponential as a special case)

Example 16 (Exponential as a special case) With \(\alpha= 1\), Theorem 13 gives \(\operatorname{h}(t) = {\lambda}\) and

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} &= \Gamma(2){\lambda}^{-1} \\ &= 1/{\lambda}, \end{aligned} \]

matching the exponential distribution’s constant hazard and mean. With \(\alpha= 2\) and \({\lambda}= 1\), \(\operatorname{h}(t) = 2t\) increases with \(t\), and

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[T\right]\mathclose{}} &= \Gamma(3/2) \\ &= \sqrt{\pi}/2 \\ &\approx 0.886. \end{aligned} \]

7 The multivariate normal distribution

DefDefinition 9 (Multivariate normal distribution (multivariate Gaussian distribution))

Definition 9 (Multivariate normal distribution (multivariate Gaussian distribution)) A \(p \times 1\) random vector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= {(X_1, \ldots, X_p)}^{\top}\) has the multivariate normal distribution (or multivariate Gaussian distribution) with mean parameter \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\) and variance parameter \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\), a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix, written \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\), if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\) has joint density:

\[ \operatorname{p}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2} \det(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{1/2}} \text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2} {(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}^{\top} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}, \quad \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\in \mathbb{R}^p \]

where \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma})\) is the determinant of \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) and \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is its inverse.

RemarkRemark

Remark. A joint density of \(p\) random variables is the \(p\)-variable analogue of a joint density of two: a non-negative function on \(\mathbb{R}^p\) whose integral over any region \(A \subseteq \mathbb{R}^p\) is \(\Pr(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\in A)\).

The density is well defined because \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is positive definite:

  • \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\) exists (positive definite matrices have positive definite inverses), and
  • \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}) > 0\) (positive definite matrices have positive determinants), so its square root is a positive number.
LemTheorem 15 (The multivariate normal density integrates to 1)

Theorem 15 (The multivariate normal density integrates to 1) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\) (Definition 9), then the density in Definition 9 integrates to 1 over \(\mathbb{R}^p\).

LemTheorem 16 (The mean and variance of the multivariate normal distribution)

Theorem 16 (The mean and variance of the multivariate normal distribution) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\) (Definition 9), then \(\mathbb{E}\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\) (the expectation of a random vector), and \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathbf{\Sigma}\) (the variance of a random vector).

RemarkRemark

Remark. The proof changes variables in a \(p\)-dimensional integral, which these notes do not develop; see [7]. As with the univariate normal distribution, these facts are where the parameters’ names come from. They also show why \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) must at least be positive semidefinite; requiring it to be positive definite rules out cases like a variance matrix that is not positive definite, which has no joint density.

ExmExample 17 (The univariate normal is the case \(p = 1\))

Example 17 (The univariate normal is the case \(p = 1\)) With \(p = 1\), \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} = (\mu)\), and \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = (\sigma^2)\) for \(\sigma^2> 0\), \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}) = \sigma^2\) (determinant, \(p = 1\)) and \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} = (1/\sigma^2)\), so:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{p}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2} (\sigma^2)^{1/2}} \text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2} (x - \mu) \frac{1}{\sigma^2} (x - \mu)} && \text{(substitute into the multivariate normal density)} \\ &= \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \text{e}^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} && \text{(simplify)} \end{aligned} \]

which is the normal density \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu, \sigma^2\right)\mathclose{}}\).

LemLemma 1 (The quadratic form in the multivariate normal density is non-negative)

Lemma 1 (The quadratic form in the multivariate normal density is non-negative) If \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), then

\[{(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}^{\top} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}) \ge 0,\]

with equality if and only if \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\).

ProofProof

Proof. \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is positive definite (positive definite inverse). If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\neq \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} \neq \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), so the quadratic form is positive by the definition of positive definite. If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\), then \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\), and the quadratic form is \(0\).

DefDefinition 10 (Mahalanobis distance)

Definition 10 (Mahalanobis distance) For a \(p \times p\) positive definite matrix \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} \in \mathbb{R}^p\), the Mahalanobis distance from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is:

\[ \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sqrt{{(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}^{\top} \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})} \]

RemarkRemark

Remark. The square root is defined by Lemma 1. See also [8].

CorCorollary 3 (The multivariate normal density decreases with Mahalanobis distance)

Corollary 3 (The multivariate normal density decreases with Mahalanobis distance) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\), then:

\[ \operatorname{p}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2} \det(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{1/2}} \text{e}^{-\frac{\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2}{2}} \]

where \(\Delta\) is the Mahalanobis distance to \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\) with respect to \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) (Definition 10). So the density is highest at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\), decreases as \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) increases, and is the same at every \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}\) with the same \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\).

ProofProof

Proof. Substitute Definition 10 into Definition 9. The function \(\delta\mapsto \text{e}^{-\delta^2/2}\) decreases on \(\delta\ge 0\), and by Lemma 1, \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 0\) only at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\).

ExmExample 18 (Mahalanobis distance for diagonal variance matrices)

Example 18 (Mahalanobis distance for diagonal variance matrices) If \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal with diagonal elements \(\sigma^2_1, \ldots, \sigma^2_p\), all positive, then \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\) is diagonal with diagonal elements \(1/\sigma^2_1, \ldots, 1/\sigma^2_p\) (multiplying the two gives \(\mathbf{I}_p\); matrix inverse), so:

\[ \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^p\frac{(x_i - \mu_i)^2}{\sigma^2_i} \]

Two special cases:

  • If \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{I}_p\), then \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^p(x_i - \mu_i)^2\), so the Mahalanobis distance is the ordinary (Euclidean) distance.
  • If \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \sigma^2\mathbf{I}_p\), then \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})\) is the Euclidean distance divided by \(\sigma\).

In general, each coordinate’s difference is measured in units of that coordinate’s standard deviation.

LemTheorem 17 (A multivariate normal vector with diagonal variance has independent normal components)

Theorem 17 (A multivariate normal vector with diagonal variance has independent normal components) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal with diagonal elements \(\sigma^2_1, \ldots, \sigma^2_p\), then:

  • the joint density of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\) is the product of the normal densities \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\), \(i = 1, \ldots, p\),
  • each \(X_i \sim {\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\), and
  • \(X_1, \ldots, X_p\) are independent.
ProofProof

Proof. By the determinant of a diagonal matrix, \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}) = \prod_{i=1}^p \sigma^2_i\), so \(\det(\mathbf{\Sigma})^{1/2} = \prod_{i=1}^p \sigma_i\). Using Example 18 for the quadratic form:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{p}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) &= \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{p/2} \prod_{i=1}^p \sigma_i} \text{e}^{-\frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^p\frac{(x_i - \mu_i)^2}{\sigma^2_i}} && \text{(substitute)} \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^p \frac{1}{\sigma_i \sqrt{2\pi}} \text{e}^{-\frac{(x_i - \mu_i)^2}{2\sigma^2_i}} && \text{(} \text{e}^{a + b} = \text{e}^{a}\text{e}^{b} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

which is the product of the \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\) densities. Integrating out every coordinate except \(x_i\), each other factor integrates to 1 (the normal density integrates to 1), leaving the \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(\mu_i, \sigma^2_i\right)\mathclose{}}\) density as the density of \(X_i\) (marginal density from a joint density, with \(p\) variables). So the joint density is the product of the marginal densities, and the components are independent by the factorization theorem for densities, extended to \(p\) variables as its remark describes.

CorCorollary 4 (For a multivariate normal vector, uncorrelated components are independent)

Corollary 4 (For a multivariate normal vector, uncorrelated components are independent) If \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\sim \operatorname{N}_p\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\), then \(X_1, \ldots, X_p\) are independent if and only if \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal, that is, if and only if \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{}} = 0\) for every \(i \neq j\).

ProofProof

Proof. By Theorem 16, \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{X}}\vphantom{X}}\right)\mathclose{}} = \mathbf{\Sigma}\), whose \((i,j)\)-th element is \({\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{}}\) (elements of the variance matrix). If \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal, the components are independent by Theorem 17. If the components are independent, then:

  • each pair \(X_i, X_j\) with \(i \neq j\) is independent,
  • each \(X_i\) is continuous, with the density left after integrating out the other coordinates of the joint density (marginal density from a joint density, with \(p\) variables), and
  • \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X_i^2\right]\mathclose{}} < \infty\), because \({\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X_i\right)\mathclose{}}\), the \((i,i)\)-th element of \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\), is defined, and a variance is defined only when \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X_i^2\right]\mathclose{}} < \infty\).

So \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) is diagonal by independent components give a diagonal variance matrix.

RemarkRemark

Remark. In general, uncorrelated random variables need not be independent (an example). Corollary 4 needs the whole vector to be multivariate normal: two random variables that are each normal and uncorrelated can still be dependent (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normally_distributed_and_uncorrelated_does_not_imply_independent).

LemTheorem 18 (Mahalanobis distance in eigenvector coordinates)

Theorem 18 (Mahalanobis distance in eigenvector coordinates) Let \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) be positive definite, with eigendecomposition \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\), where \(\mathbf{Q}\) has columns \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1, \ldots, \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_p\) and \(\mathbf{\Lambda}\) has diagonal elements \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_p\). Then:

\[ \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 = \sum_{i=1}^p\frac{\mathopen{}\left({\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i}^{\top}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})\right)\mathclose{}^2}{\lambda_i} \]

ProofProof

Proof. Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} = {\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})\), whose \(i\)-th element is \(y_i = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i}^{\top}(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})\). By the inverse of a positive definite matrix, \(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top}\), with every \(\lambda_i > 0\). So:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 &= {(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}^{\top} \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1}{\mathbf{Q}}^{\top} (\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}) && \text{(definition; substitute } \mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1} \text{)} \\ &= {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}} && \text{(} {(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}})}^{\top}\mathbf{Q} = {\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{y}}\vphantom{y}}}^{\top} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^p\frac{y_i^2}{\lambda_i} && \text{(} \mathbf{\Lambda}^{-1} \text{ is diagonal)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark

Remark. \(y_i\) is the coordinate of \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}- \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\) along the eigenvector \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\). So the set of points at Mahalanobis distance \(\delta\) from \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\), where the multivariate normal density is constant (Corollary 3), is an ellipse (\(p = 2\)) or ellipsoid centered at \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}}\), with axes along the eigenvectors of \(\mathbf{\Sigma}\) and half-length \(\delta\sqrt{\lambda_i}\) along \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_i\).

ExmExample 19 (Elliptical contours of a bivariate normal density)

Example 19 (Elliptical contours of a bivariate normal density) Let \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{\mu}}\vphantom{\mu}} = \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}\) and \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}\), which has eigenvalues \(3\) and \(1\), with eigenvectors \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{(1, 1)}^{\top}\) and \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}{(1, -1)}^{\top}\) (an eigendecomposition example). By Theorem 18:

\[ \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 = \frac{(x_1 + x_2)^2}{2 \cdot 3} + \frac{(x_1 - x_2)^2}{2 \cdot 1} \]

The point

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}&= \sqrt{3}\,\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_1 \\ &= \sqrt{3/2}\,{(1, 1)}^{\top} \end{aligned} \]

has \(x_1 + x_2 = \sqrt{6}\) and \(x_1 - x_2 = 0\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 &= 6/6 \\ &= 1; \end{aligned} \]

the point \(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}= \mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{q}}\vphantom{q}}_2\) has \(x_1 + x_2 = 0\) and \(x_1 - x_2 = \sqrt{2}\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} \Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}})^2 &= 2/2 \\ &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

So the contour \(\Delta(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{x}}\vphantom{x}}) = 1\) of the \(\operatorname{N}_2\mathopen{}\left(\mathord{\smash[b]{\underset{\raise.3ex\hbox{$\scriptstyle\sim$}}{0}}\vphantom{0}}, \mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\mathclose{}\) density is an ellipse stretched along \({(1, 1)}^{\top}\), with half-length \(\sqrt{3}\), and squeezed along \({(1, -1)}^{\top}\), with half-length \(1\): \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) are positively correlated (Figure 4).

8 Mixture distributions

DefDefinition 11 (Mixture density)

Definition 11 (Mixture density) Let \({\operatorname{p}_1}, \ldots, {\operatorname{p}_K}\) be densities on \(\mathbb{R}\), and let \(w_1, \ldots, w_K\) be numbers with:

  • \(w_c \ge 0\) for every \(c\), and
  • \(\sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c = 1\).

The mixture of \({\operatorname{p}_1}, \ldots, {\operatorname{p}_K}\) with mixing weights \(w_1, \ldots, w_K\) is the function [9]:

\[ {\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x), \quad x \in \mathbb{R} \]

DefDefinition 12 (Mixture component)

Definition 12 (Mixture component) The densities \({\operatorname{p}_1}, \ldots, {\operatorname{p}_K}\) in a mixture (Definition 11) are its components.

LemTheorem 19 (A mixture is the density of a two-stage random variable)

Theorem 19 (A mixture is the density of a two-stage random variable) Let \(C\) be a discrete random variable and \(X\) a continuous random variable with joint density-mass function

\[\operatorname{p}(C = c,\, X = x) = w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x), \quad c \in {\mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, K\right\}\mathclose{}},\]

with \({\operatorname{p}_c}\) and \(w_c\) as in Definition 11. Then:

  • \(\mathbb{P}(C = c) = w_c\),
  • for each \(c\) with \(w_c > 0\), the conditional density of \(X\) given \(C = c\) is \({\operatorname{p}_c}\), and
  • the mixture \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}\) is a density of \(X\).
ProofProof

Proof. By the marginal PMF from a joint density-mass function,

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C = c) &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx \\ &= w_c, \end{aligned} \]

because \({\operatorname{p}_c}\) is a density and so integrates to 1. Then, by the definition of the conditional density,

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{p}(X = x \mid C = c) &= w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x) / w_c \\ &= {\operatorname{p}_c}(x). \end{aligned} \]

For the last claim, the events \({\mathopen{}\left\{C = 1\right\}\mathclose{}}, \ldots, {\mathopen{}\left\{C = K\right\}\mathclose{}}\) are mutually exclusive, and their union is the whole sample space. So for any interval \(B\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr(X \in B) &= \sum_{c=1}^{K} \Pr(C = c,\, X \in B) && \text{(additivity over the events } {\mathopen{}\left\{C = c\right\}\mathclose{}} \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{c=1}^{K} \int_B w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx && \text{(definition of a joint density-mass function)} \\ &= \int_B \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx && \text{(a finite sum of integrals is the integral of the sum)} \\ &= \int_B {\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x)\,dx && \text{(definition of a mixture)} \end{aligned} \]

And \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}} \ge 0\), as a sum of non-negative terms, so \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}\) is a density of \(X\).

RemarkRemark

Remark. Theorem 19 describes how to generate a draw from a mixture: first draw a component label \(C\) with \(\mathbb{P}(C = c) = w_c\), then draw \(X\) from the component density \({\operatorname{p}_C}\). In applications, \(C\) is often an unobserved subpopulation, such as a disease subtype. Mixtures of multivariate densities, such as mixtures of multivariate normal densities (Gaussian mixture models), work the same way, with \(p\)-variable densities in place of densities on \(\mathbb{R}\).

LemTheorem 20 (The mean of a mixture is the weighted mean of the component means)

Theorem 20 (The mean of a mixture is the weighted mean of the component means) If \(X\) has density \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}\) (Definition 11), and each component \({\operatorname{p}_c}\) has a defined mean \(\mu_c = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx\), then:

\[{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}} = \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \, \mu_c\]

ProofProof

Proof. The integral defining \({\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}}\) converges absolutely, because \(\int_{-\infty}^\infty \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{} \, {\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x)\,dx = \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \int_{-\infty}^\infty \mathopen{}\left|x\right|\mathclose{} \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx\), a finite sum of finite numbers. Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}} &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, {\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x)\,dx && \text{(definition of expectation)} \\ &= \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx && \text{(definition of a mixture)} \\ &= \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\,dx && \text{(a finite sum of integrals is the integral of the sum)} \\ &= \sum_{c=1}^{K} w_c \, \mu_c && \text{(definition of } \mu_c \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The first step is the definition of expectation.

LemTheorem 21 (Which component did an observation come from?)

Theorem 21 (Which component did an observation come from?) Under the conditions of Theorem 19, for each \(x\) with \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x) > 0\):

\[ \mathbb{P}(C = c \mid X = x) = \frac{w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \, {\operatorname{p}_k}(x)} \]

ProofProof

Proof. By Theorem 19, \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}\) is a density of \(X\). By the definition of the conditional PMF (the case \(X\) continuous, \(C\) discrete):

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C = c \mid X = x) &= \frac{\operatorname{p}(C = c,\, X = x)}{\operatorname{p}(X = x)} && \text{(definition of the conditional PMF)} \\ &= \frac{w_c \, {\operatorname{p}_c}(x)}{\sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k \, {\operatorname{p}_k}(x)} && \text{(substitute the joint density-mass function and } {\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

RemarkRemark

Remark. Theorem 21 has the form of Bayes’ theorem, with densities in place of some probabilities: the prior probability \(w_c\) of component \(c\) is multiplied by the component density \({\operatorname{p}_c}(x)\) at the observation and divided by the overall density \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(x)\), which plays the role of the law of total probability.

DefDefinition 13 (Responsibility of a mixture component)

Definition 13 (Responsibility of a mixture component) In the setting of Theorem 21, the conditional probability \(\mathbb{P}(C = c \mid X = x)\) is called, in machine learning, the responsibility of component \(c\) for \(x\).

ExmExample 20 (A two-component normal mixture)

Example 20 (A two-component normal mixture) Let \({\operatorname{p}_1}\) be the \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, 1\right)\mathclose{}}\) density and \({\operatorname{p}_2}\) the \({\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(6, 1\right)\mathclose{}}\) density, with mixing weights \(w_1 = 0.3\) and \(w_2 = 0.7\), and let \(X\) have density \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}} = 0.3\,{\operatorname{p}_1} + 0.7\,{\operatorname{p}_2}\).

  • Mean. By Theorem 20 and the normal means,

    \[ \begin{aligned} {\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}} &= 0.3 \cdot 0 + 0.7 \cdot 6 \\ &= 4.2. \end{aligned} \]

  • Density. \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}\) has peaks near the two component means, \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(0) \approx 0.120\) and \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(6) \approx 0.279\), with a trough between them, \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(3) \approx 0.004\). The density at the mean, \({\operatorname{p}_{\text{mix}}}(4.2) \approx 0.055\), is less than half its value at either peak.

  • Responsibilities. By Theorem 21, at \(x = 3\), halfway between the component means, \({\operatorname{p}_1}(3) = {\operatorname{p}_2}(3)\) by the symmetry of the normal density, so:

    \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(C = 2 \mid X = 3) &= \frac{0.7\,{\operatorname{p}_2}(3)}{0.3\,{\operatorname{p}_1}(3) + 0.7\,{\operatorname{p}_2}(3)} \\ &= \frac{0.7}{0.3 + 0.7} \\ &= 0.7, \end{aligned} \]

    the prior weight: an observation equally far from both components carries no information about which component it came from. At \(x = 4.2\), the same formula gives \(\mathbb{P}(C = 2 \mid X = 4.2) \approx 0.9997\).

RemarkRemark

Remark. Example 20 shows that the mean of a mixture can fall where observations are rare, so a single normal distribution fitted to such data would describe them poorly.

ExmExample 21 (The negative binomial as a continuous mixture)

Example 21 (The negative binomial as a continuous mixture) The negative binomial distribution is a mixture of Poisson distributions with a continuum of components, one for each mean \({\lambda}> 0\): its PMF is \(\mathbb{P}(Y = y) = \int_0^\infty g({\lambda})\,\frac{{\lambda}^y e^{-{\lambda}}}{y!}\,d{\lambda}\), where the gamma density \(g\) plays the role of the mixing weights and the integral replaces the sum in Definition 11 (see the proof of Theorem 11).
