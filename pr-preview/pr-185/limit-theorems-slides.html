1 The Central Limit Theorem

Remark. The average of many independent random variables, none of which dominates the others, has a distribution that is approximately bell-shaped, whatever the distributions of the individual variables. Theorem 1 makes this precise.

Theorem 1 (Central Limit Theorem) Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be IID random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2> 0\), and let \(\bar{X}_{n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Then for every real number \(z\):

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}{\sigma} \le z\right)\mathclose{} = \Phi(z) \]

where \(\Phi(z) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\int_{-\infty}^{z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \text{e}^{-u^2/2}\,du\) is the CDF of the standard normal distribution \(\operatorname{N}\mathopen{}\left(0, 1\right)\mathclose{}\).

Remark. This version is the Lindeberg–Lévy CLT; its proof is beyond these notes’ scope (Billingsley 1995, Theorem 27.1). Other versions relax the IID assumption, which is why the informal statement asks only that no summand dominate.

Corollary 1 (Central Limit Theorem for a sum) Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be IID random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2> 0\), and let \(S_n \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Then for every real number \(z\):

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \le z\right)\mathclose{} = \Phi(z) \]

Proof. Since \(S_n = n\bar{X}_{n}\),

\[ \frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{n(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}{\sigma}, \]

so the event in this corollary is the event in Theorem 1, and the limits agree.

Corollary 2 (Mean and variance of a sum of IID random variables) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be IID random variables, each discrete or continuous, with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), and let \(S_n \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Then:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[S_n\right]\mathclose{} &= n\mu\\ \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S_n\right)\mathclose{} &= n\sigma^2 \end{aligned} \]

Proof. For the mean, apply linearity of expectation once per added summand:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[S_n\right]\mathclose{} &= \sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X_i\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(linearity of expectation, applied } n - 1 \text{ times)} \\ &= n\mu && \text{(each } X_i \text{ has mean } \mu\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

For the variance, apply the variance of a linear combination with every \(a_i = 1\). For \(i \ne j\), \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) are independent (take \(A_k = \mathbb{R}\) for every other \(k\) in the definition of independence), so \(\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{} = 0\) for independent summands; and \(\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_i\right)\mathclose{} = \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X_i\right)\mathclose{}\) (covariance of a variable with itself):

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S_n\right)\mathclose{} &= \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n \operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(variance of a linear combination, all } a_i = 1 \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_i\right)\mathclose{} + \sum_{\substack{i, j = 1 \\ i \ne j}}^n \operatorname{Cov}\mathopen{}\left(X_i, X_j\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(split off the terms with } i = j \text{)} \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(X_i\right)\mathclose{} + 0 && \text{(covariance with itself; independent summands)} \\ &= n\sigma^2 && \text{(each } X_i \text{ has variance } \sigma^2\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Remark. In practice, Theorem 1 justifies approximating \(\bar{X}_{n}\) by a normal distribution with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2/n\), and Corollary 1 justifies approximating \(S_n\) by a normal distribution with mean \(n\mu\) and variance \(n\sigma^2\). By Corollary 2, these are exactly the means and variances of \(\bar{X}_{n} = S_n/n\) and \(S_n\).

Example 1 (The sum of five dice) A single fair die roll has the discrete uniform distribution on \(\mathopen{}\left\{1, \ldots, 6\right\}\mathclose{}\), which is flat, not bell-shaped (Figure 1). Its mean is \(\mu= 3.5\), and its variance is

\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2&= \sum_{x=1}^{6} (x - 3.5)^2 / 6 \\ &= 35/12. \end{aligned} \]

Example 2 (Sample means of car horsepower) The Auto data record the horsepower and fuel economy of 392 car models sold between 1970 and 1982. They come from the StatLib library at Carnegie Mellon University and ship with the book An Introduction to Statistical Learning, in the Python package ISLP (ISLP.load_data("Auto")) and the R package ISLR2 (ISLR2::Auto). This site has a copy at data/auto.csv, and both code tabs below read that file.

Horsepower is not bell-shaped: a few powerful cars give it a long right tail. To apply Theorem 1, treat each draw of one car at random as one IID observation \(X_i\), so that \(\mu\) and \(\sigma^2\) are the mean and variance of the 392 horsepower values. The sample mean \(\bar{X}_{n} = S_n/n\), where \(S_n = \sum_{i=1}^nX_i\), has mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), because \(S_n\) has mean \(n\mu\) and variance \(n\sigma^2\) by Corollary 2. The code below draws 10,000 random samples of each size \(n \in \mathopen{}\left\{1, 5, 30\right\}\mathclose{}\), with replacement, and computes each sample’s mean.

2 Tail bounds

Theorem 2 (Markov’s inequality) Let \(X\) be a random variable with \(X \ge 0\) and \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{} < \infty\). Then for every \(a > 0\):

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(X \ge a\right)\mathclose{} \le \frac{\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{}}{a} \]

Proof. Because \(X \ge 0\), every outcome satisfies \(X \ge a \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\mathopen{}\left\{X \ge a\right\}\mathclose{}}\): when \(X \ge a\) the right side is \(a \le X\), and otherwise it is \(0 \le X\). The expectation of a nonnegative random variable is nonnegative, so by linearity of expectation, and because the expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event,

\[ \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{} - a \cdot \Pr\mathopen{}\left(X \ge a\right)\mathclose{} = \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X - a \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\mathopen{}\left\{X \ge a\right\}\mathclose{}}\right]\mathclose{} \ge 0 \]

and dividing by \(a > 0\) gives the claim.

Remark. The inequality carries Markov’s name by convention. Chebyshev’s inequality, which follows from it, is older (Bienaymé 1853; Tchébychef 1867); see (Seneta 2013) for the history of the attribution. A modern treatment is in (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019).

Theorem 3 (Chebyshev’s inequality) Let \(X\) be a random variable with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2> 0\). Then for every \(k > 0\):

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|X - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge k\sigma\right)\mathclose{} \le \frac{1}{k^2} \]

Proof. The random variable \((X - \mu)^2\) is nonnegative, and its expectation is \(\sigma^2\). Because \(\mathopen{}\left|X - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge k\sigma\) exactly when \((X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2\), Theorem 2 gives

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|X - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge k\sigma\right)\mathclose{} = \Pr\mathopen{}\left((X - \mu)^2 \ge k^2\sigma^2\right)\mathclose{} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{k^2\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{k^2} \]

Remark. Theorem 3 needs only a mean and a variance, not a distribution, so it holds for every distribution that has them. That generality makes it loose: for a normal distribution, the chance of landing more than two standard deviations from the mean is about 5%, but the bound at \(k = 2\) is 25%.

Remark. Bienaymé derived this bound in 1853 (Bienaymé 1853), and Chebyshev proved a more general form in 1867 (Tchébychef 1867). A modern treatment is in (Blitzstein and Hwang 2019).

Example 3 (How loose is Chebyshev’s bound for car horsepower?) Take the 392 horsepower values in the Auto data described in Example 2 as the whole population, and draw one car at random. For each \(k\), the code below counts the share of cars whose horsepower is at least \(k\) standard deviations from the mean, and sets it beside the bound \(1/k^2\) from Theorem 3.

hp <- read.csv("data/auto.csv")$horsepower
mu <- mean(hp)
sigma <- sqrt(mean((hp - mu)^2))

k <- c(1.5, 2, 3)
share_beyond <- sapply(k, function(kk) mean(abs(hp - mu) >= kk * sigma))
data.frame(k = k, share_of_cars = share_beyond, chebyshev_bound = 1 / k^2)

The bound holds at every \(k\), as Theorem 3 guarantees. At \(k = 2\) the share of cars is 4.3%, against a bound of 25%, and at \(k = 3\) it is 1.3% against 11.1%. The bound is loose because it must hold for every distribution with this mean and variance.

Exercise 1 (How many cars to estimate average fuel economy) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be IID draws of the fuel economy (mpg) of a car chosen at random from the Auto data, with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), and let \(\bar{X}_{n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\).

  1. Use Corollary 2 and Theorem 3 to show that for every \(\epsilon > 0\),

    \[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \epsilon\right)\mathclose{} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} \]

  2. Compute \(\sigma^2\) for the mpg column of data/auto.csv, treating the 392 cars as the whole population. Find the smallest \(n\) for which the bound in (a) guarantees \(\Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge 1\right)\mathclose{} \le 0.05\).

  3. Simulate 10,000 samples of that size drawn with replacement, and count how many sample means miss \(\mu\) by 1 or more. Why is the count so far below the 5% the bound allows?

Solution 1.

  1. By Corollary 2, \(\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S_n\right)\mathclose{} = n\sigma^2\) for \(S_n \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\), so \(\bar{X}_{n} = S_n/n\) has mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2/n\) (a constant factor \(c\) scales a variance by \(c^2\)). Apply Theorem 3 to \(\bar{X}_{n}\) with \(k\sigma/\sqrt{n} = \epsilon\), that is, \(k = \epsilon\sqrt{n}/\sigma\), so \(1/k^2 = \sigma^2/(n\epsilon^2)\).

  2. Setting \(\sigma^2/(n \cdot 1^2) \le 0.05\) gives \(n \ge 20\sigma^2\).

  3. See the code below.

mpg <- read.csv("data/auto.csv")$mpg
mu <- mean(mpg)
sigma2 <- mean((mpg - mu)^2)
n_min <- ceiling(20 * sigma2)

set.seed(571)
xbar <- replicate(10000, mean(sample(mpg, n_min, replace = TRUE)))
misses <- sum(abs(xbar - mu) >= 1)
normal_approx <- 2 * pnorm(-1 / sqrt(sigma2 / n_min))
c(variance = sigma2, n = n_min, misses = misses, normal_approx = normal_approx)

The population variance of mpg is 60.8, so \(n = 1216\) cars suffice. Out of 10,000 samples of that size, 0 sample means missed \(\mu\) by 1 or more, against the 5% (500 samples) the bound allows. The bound is loose because the sample mean of this many cars is nearly normal (Theorem 1), and the normal approximation puts the chance of a miss at about 7.7^{-6}; Chebyshev’s inequality must hold for every distribution, as in Example 3.

Theorem 4 (Chebyshev’s inequality for a sample average) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be IID random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2> 0\), and let \(\bar{X}_{n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Then for every \(\epsilon > 0\):

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \epsilon\right)\mathclose{} \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\epsilon^2} \]

Proof. This is the solution to Exercise 1 (a), worked in Solution 1. It applies Theorem 3 to \(\bar{X}_{n}\), whose variance is \(\sigma^2/n\) by Corollary 2.

Lemma 1 (Hoeffding’s lemma) Let \(X\) be a random variable with \(0 \le X \le 1\) and mean \(\mu\). Then for every real number \(s\),

\[ \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{s(X - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} \le e^{s^2/8}. \]

Proof. First bound \(e^{sX}\) by a line. The function \(x \mapsto e^{x}\) is convex, and for \(0 \le X \le 1\) the number \(sX\) is the weighted average \((1 - X) \cdot 0 + X \cdot s\), so

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{sX} &\le (1 - X) e^{0} + X e^{s} && \text{(convexity of the exponential)} \\ &= 1 - X + X e^{s} && \text{(} e^{0} = 1 \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Take the expectation of both sides, which preserves the inequality:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{sX}\right]\mathclose{} &\le 1 - \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{} + \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[X\right]\mathclose{} e^{s} && \text{(linearity of expectation)} \\ &= 1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s} && \text{(definition of } \mu\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The number \(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\) is a weighted average of \(1\) and \(e^{s}\) with weights \(1 - \mu\) and \(\mu\), so it is positive. Multiply by the constant \(e^{-s\mu}\) and write the result as an exponential:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{s(X - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} &= e^{-s\mu} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{sX}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(} e^{-s\mu} \text{ is a constant)} \\ &\le e^{-s\mu} \mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(the bound above)} \\ &= e^{L(s)} && \text{(define } L(s) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}-s\mu+ \log\mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\right)\mathclose{} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

It remains to show \(L(s) \le s^2/8\). Write

\[ p(s) \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{\mu e^{s}}{1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}}, \]

which lies in \([0, 1]\). The first derivative of \(L\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} L'(s) &= -\mu+ \frac{\mu e^{s}}{1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}} && \text{(chain rule for the logarithm)} \\ &= -\mu+ p(s) && \text{(definition of } p \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The derivative of \(p\) is

\[ \begin{aligned} p'(s) &= \frac{\mu e^{s}\mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\right)\mathclose{} - \mu e^{s} \cdot \mu e^{s}}{\mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\right)\mathclose{}^2} && \text{(quotient rule)} \\ &= \frac{\mu e^{s}\mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu\right)\mathclose{}}{\mathopen{}\left(1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}\right)\mathclose{}^2} && \text{(the } \mu^2 e^{2s} \text{ terms cancel)} \\ &= p(s)\mathopen{}\left(1 - p(s)\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(} 1 - p(s) = \tfrac{1 - \mu}{1 - \mu+ \mu e^{s}} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

so \(L''(s) = p'(s) = p(s)\mathopen{}\left(1 - p(s)\right)\mathclose{}\). Completing the square gives

\[ \begin{aligned} p(s)\mathopen{}\left(1 - p(s)\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{1}{4} - \mathopen{}\left(p(s) - \frac{1}{2}\right)\mathclose{}^2 && \text{(complete the square)} \\ &\le \frac{1}{4} && \text{(a square is not negative)} \end{aligned} \]

At \(s = 0\), \(L(0) = 0 + \log 1 = 0\) and \(L'(0) = -\mu+ \mu= 0\). By Taylor’s theorem with the Lagrange remainder, there is a number \(\xi\) between \(0\) and \(s\) with

\[ \begin{aligned} L(s) &= L(0) + L'(0)\, s + \frac{L''(\xi)}{2} s^2 && \text{(Taylor's theorem)} \\ &= \frac{L''(\xi)}{2} s^2 && \text{(} L(0) = L'(0) = 0 \text{)} \\ &\le \frac{s^2}{8} && \text{(} L''(\xi) \le \tfrac{1}{4} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

Therefore \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{s(X - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} \le e^{L(s)} \le e^{s^2/8}\).

Remark. The bound on the moment generating function \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{s(X - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{}\) is the step of Hoeffding’s 1963 paper (Hoeffding 1963) that the literature calls Hoeffding’s lemma. The next result uses it to bound the tails of an average.

Theorem 5 (Hoeffding’s inequality) Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be IID random variables with \(0 \le X_i \le 1\) and mean \(\mu\), and let \(\bar{X}_{n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\). Then for every \(\varepsilon> 0\),

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} \le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}. \tag{1}\]

Proof. Fix \(s > 0\). The event \(\mathopen{}\left\{\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right\}\mathclose{}\) is the event \(\mathopen{}\left\{e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)} \ge e^{sn\varepsilon}\right\}\mathclose{}\), because the exponential is increasing and \(sn > 0\). The random variable \(e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}\) is positive, so Markov’s inequality applies:

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} &= \Pr\mathopen{}\left(e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)} \ge e^{sn\varepsilon}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(the exponential is increasing)} \\ &\le e^{-sn\varepsilon} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(Markov's inequality)} \end{aligned} \]

Rewrite the exponent as a sum, so that the exponential becomes a product:

\[ \begin{aligned} e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)} &= e^{s\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i - \mu)} && \text{(} n\bar{X}_{n} = \sum_{i=1}^nX_i \text{)} \\ &= \prod_{i=1}^n e^{s(X_i - \mu)} && \text{(} e^{a + b} = e^{a} e^{b} \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The factors are functions of independent random variables, so they are independent, and the expectation of a product of independent random variables is the product of their expectations (the expectation of a product of independent variables, applied one factor at a time). Then apply Lemma 1 to each factor:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{sn(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} &= \prod_{i=1}^n \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[e^{s(X_i - \mu)}\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(independence)} \\ &\le \prod_{i=1}^n e^{s^2/8} && \text{(Hoeffding's lemma)} \\ &= e^{ns^2/8} && \text{(} n \text{ equal factors)} \end{aligned} \]

Combining the two displays, for every \(s > 0\),

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} \le e^{-sn\varepsilon+ ns^2/8}. \]

The right side is smallest where the exponent \(-sn\varepsilon+ ns^2/8\) is smallest. The exponent is a quadratic in \(s\) with a positive leading coefficient, so it has one minimum, where its derivative is zero. Its derivative in \(s\) is \(-n\varepsilon+ ns/4\), which is zero at \(s = 4\varepsilon\), and \(4\varepsilon> 0\) is allowed. There the exponent is

\[ \begin{aligned} -4\varepsilon\cdot n\varepsilon+ \frac{n(4\varepsilon)^2}{8} &= -4n\varepsilon^2 + 2n\varepsilon^2 && \text{(substitute } s = 4\varepsilon\text{)} \\ &= -2n\varepsilon^2 && \text{(combine like terms)} \end{aligned} \]

which is the claim.

Corollary 3 (Hoeffding’s inequality, two-sided) Under the assumptions of Theorem 5, for every \(\varepsilon> 0\),

\[ \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} \le 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}. \tag{2}\]

Proof. The variables \(1 - X_1, \ldots, 1 - X_n\) are IID, lie in \([0, 1]\), and have mean \(1 - \mu\). Their average is \(1 - \bar{X}_{n}\), so Theorem 5 applied to them gives

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mu- \bar{X}_{n} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} &= \Pr\mathopen{}\left((1 - \bar{X}_{n}) - (1 - \mu) \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(rearrange the event)} \\ &\le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} && \text{(Hoeffding's inequality for } 1 - X_i \text{)} \end{aligned} \]

The event \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \varepsilon\right\}\mathclose{}\) is the union of \(\mathopen{}\left\{\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right\}\mathclose{}\) and \(\mathopen{}\left\{\mu- \bar{X}_{n} \ge \varepsilon\right\}\mathclose{}\). The probability of a union is at most the sum of the probabilities, so

\[ \begin{aligned} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} &\le \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} + \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mu- \bar{X}_{n} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(union bound)} \\ &\le e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} + e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} && \text{(Hoeffding's inequality, twice)} \\ &= 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2} && \text{(combine like terms)} \end{aligned} \]

Remark. Hoeffding stated the inequality for sums of independent bounded random variables, with different bounds for each term (Hoeffding 1963). This page states the case of an average of IID variables in \([0, 1]\), which is the case that bounds the gap between a sample proportion and the probability it estimates. For an indicator variable, \(\mu\) is the probability of the event and \(\bar{X}_{n}\) is the share of the \(n\) draws in which it occurs.

Example 4 (How loose is Hoeffding’s bound for the share of efficient cars?) Take the 392 cars in the Auto data described in Example 2 as the whole population, and call a car efficient if its fuel economy (mpg) is above \(25\). Let \(X_i = 1\) if the \(i\)th car drawn at random is efficient and \(X_i = 0\) otherwise, so \(\mu\) is the share of efficient cars and \(\bar{X}_{n}\) is the share in a sample of \(n\) draws. Each \(X_i\) lies in \([0, 1]\), and the draws are made with replacement, so the \(X_i\) are IID and Corollary 3 applies. The code below draws \(10{,}000\) samples of each size, computes the proportion of samples whose share misses \(\mu\) by at least \(\varepsilon= 0.1\), and sets that proportion beside the bound \(2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}\) of Equation 2 and the bound \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\) that Chebyshev’s inequality gives (Theorem 4).

mpg <- read.csv("data/auto.csv")$mpg
efficient <- as.numeric(mpg > 25)
mu <- mean(efficient)
sigma2 <- mean((efficient - mu)^2)
tol <- 0.1

n <- c(50, 100, 200, 400)
set.seed(571)
share_missed <- sapply(n, function(nn) {
  xbar <- replicate(10000, mean(sample(efficient, nn, replace = TRUE)))
  mean(abs(xbar - mu) >= tol)
})
hoeffding_bound <- pmin(1, 2 * exp(-2 * n * tol^2))
chebyshev_bound <- pmin(1, sigma2 / (n * tol^2))
data.frame(
  n = n,
  share_missed = share_missed,
  hoeffding_bound = hoeffding_bound,
  chebyshev_bound = chebyshev_bound
)

The simulated proportions lie below both bounds at every sample size, as they should: the bounds apply to the true probabilities these proportions estimate. The share of efficient cars is 0.398. For \(n = 50\) and \(n = 100\) Chebyshev’s bound is the smaller of the two, but the Hoeffding bound falls exponentially in \(n\) while the Chebyshev bound falls only like \(1/n\), so at \(n = 200\) and \(n = 400\) Hoeffding’s is the smaller. At \(n = 400\) the Hoeffding bound is 0.00067, against 0.06 for Chebyshev, and 0 of the 10,000 samples missed. Both bounds are loose because they must hold for every distribution of this kind, not only for this one.

3 The law of large numbers

Definition 1 (Convergence in probability) A sequence of random variables \(Y_1, Y_2, \ldots\) converges in probability to a constant \(c\) if, for every distance \(\varepsilon > 0\), the probability that \(Y_n\) lies at least \(\varepsilon\) away from \(c\) goes to zero as \(n\) grows:

\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|Y_n - c\right|\mathclose{} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} = 0 \]

We write \(Y_n \overset{\scriptstyle p}{\longrightarrow}c\).

Source

Casella and Berger (2002, Def. 5.5.1).

Theorem 6 (Weak law of large numbers) Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be IID random variables with mean \(\mu\) and finite variance \(\sigma^2> 0\), and let \(\bar{X}_{n} \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\) be the sample mean. Then the sample mean converges in probability to \(\mu\):

\[ \bar{X}_{n} \overset{\scriptstyle p}{\longrightarrow}\mu \]

Source

Casella and Berger (2002, Thm. 5.5.2).

Proof. By Corollary 2, \(\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[S_n\right]\mathclose{} = n\mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S_n\right)\mathclose{} = n\sigma^2\), where \(S_n \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\), so \(\bar{X}_{n} = S_n / n\) has

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[\bar{X}_{n}\right]\mathclose{} &= \frac{1}{n}\operatorname{E}\mathopen{}\left[S_n\right]\mathclose{} && \text{(linearity of expectation)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n} \cdot n\mu && \text{(mean of a sum of IID variables)}\\ &= \mu && \text{(cancel $n$)}\\ \operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(\bar{X}_{n}\right)\mathclose{} &= \frac{1}{n^2}\operatorname{Var}\mathopen{}\left(S_n\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(variance of a constant multiple)}\\ &= \frac{1}{n^2} \cdot n\sigma^2 && \text{(variance of a sum of IID variables)}\\ &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n} && \text{(cancel one factor of $n$)} \end{aligned} \]

Fix \(\varepsilon > 0\) and let \(k \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\varepsilon \sqrt{n} / \sigma\), so that \(k \sigma/ \sqrt{n} = \varepsilon\). Chebyshev’s inequality for \(\bar{X}_{n}\), whose standard deviation is \(\sigma/ \sqrt{n}\), gives

\[ \begin{aligned} 0 \le \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge \varepsilon\right)\mathclose{} &= \Pr\mathopen{}\left(\mathopen{}\left|\bar{X}_{n} - \mu\right|\mathclose{} \ge k \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\mathclose{} && \text{(definition of $k$)}\\ &\le \frac{1}{k^2} && \text{(Chebyshev's inequality)}\\ &= \frac{\sigma^2}{n \varepsilon^2} && \text{(substitute $k$)} \end{aligned} \]

The bound goes to zero as \(n \to \infty\), so the probability, squeezed between zero and the bound, goes to zero. This holds for every \(\varepsilon > 0\).
